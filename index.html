Ryan Mansour

Everyone knows a picture is worth a thousand words. As a writer, I want to tell the stories of those who often fall outside the general view of our ever-changing world. Everyone is unique; everyone is different. Highlighting that difference can introduce people to a tradition they never knew existed, or a story that interests them, or one they may even relate to. The world is a vast place, and exploring the stories of people through mediums like photography is just as impactful as telling them through prose. I aim to capture important stories, those that enthral or entertain, that excite or despair, those photos that burn into your mind and stay there.

Photomontage

In my photomontage, I aimed to highlight the feeling of being caught between worlds. On the surface, my image looks like someone standing between the Great Pyramids and New York City. I chose these images because they represent me. I am the son of immigrants living in America. As much as I try to uphold the values of my family's country, I find myself growing up in a society that doesn't share them. I see people around me living lives my family warns me against. And when I see people from Egypt, it strikes me how different the reality of living here compared to there is. As a storyteller, I attempted to write a story about this a few years ago. A young man growing up in America sees how his family is ostracized and portrayed around him, choosing to reject all influences from his culture to be accepted. In the process of creating this character, I noticed how similar he was to me, and how being caught between what you are surrounded by and what is expected of you is a unique experience. In this montage, I used several blend options as well as perspective. I wanted to showcase how this person was sandwiched between two difficult places. They are a product of his surroundings, yet they are still grounded in ideals from across the globe. The skies of the images blended well initially, but I chose to highlight the difference between them even more, just to showcase how they contrast. The blend tool I used on the NYC skyline was called “Hard Light.” I used it because of how brilliant it looked. I decided on this because it gave me a “grass is not always greener” kind of feeling, which is what I wanted the viewer to feel.
