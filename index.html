Everyone knows a picture is worth a thousand words. As a writer, I want to tell the stories of those who often fall outside the general view of our ever-changing world. Everyone is unique; everyone is different. Highlighting that difference can introduce people to a tradition they never knew existed, or a story that interests them, or one they may even relate to. The world is a vast place, and exploring the stories of people through mediums like photography is just as impactful as telling them through prose. I aim to capture important stories, those that enthral or entertain, that excite or despair, those photos that burn into your mind and stay there.

Plato's Cave

Photos are an important medium in the current age, as they allow people to express themselves, share important moments, and capture history all in one. Photography as a medium is a very versatile medium, as it provides people with an opportunity to share or preserve something important to them. Susan Sontag mentions soldiers who bring home a chest full of presumed riches; when they open it, they find it filled with pictures of various things, like shopping centers, animals, and natural scenery. Sontag's description of these things as being important cannot be understated. Countless times, we are subject to a horrifying image or one that makes us question what is happening around us, and, if we are so lucky, to an image that introduces us to a new perspective, viewpoint, or reality of which we were previously unaware. My art form is writing. It is incomparable to me the vast worlds I can create, the ideologies I can capture, the principles I can question through my prose. However, through the medium of photography, you are able to capture all that in one image. The beauty of prose is something that is limited, as people may struggle to read something or not have the time, but everyone can observe an image for a minute and take away one thousand conclusions. The use of photography allows people to understand concepts or see pictures that may change their viewpoints on certain issues. For example, images coming out of Gaza, specifically recently, have severely impacted many people, of which some may have not had an opinion, or some who may have defended the violence. Photos serve a important role as they give people a visual representation of an idea rather than one that requires deeper understanding and analysis. A photo can be understood faster than a book, for example, while also containing a deep amount of nuance and context. Pictures of wildlife being destroyed, warzones/violence, or tragedies elicit a response in the viewer that something written cannot. There have been many books written about war and conflicts, but if one really wants to learn about the sheer damage in a certain area, look no further than photos. Sontag says, “The photograph is a thin slice of space as well as time.” This is true because in context many pictures meanings change. You have seen photos of the land during and after trench warfare in WWII, how animals returned to the city street when COVID had people stuck indoors, how cities have developed over time, and how those cities changed when paired with the policies of that time. Particularly I find images of urban expansion and evolution interesting. It is shocking to see how cities looked in the early days of colonialism, compared to now. There are lakes and forests where there are now buildings and roads. Similarly, seeing how neighborhoods looked like in previous decades compared to now also interests me. NYC in the 70s compared to now for example, and also how immigrant enclaves formed over time in some areas are also avaiable to see through images.

AI Slop

AI has been a main part of life since at least 2022. I remember my teachers being surprised when a student handed in an essay that earned an A, only to turn around and say it was all AI. I remember my freshman year of college here at Hunter, and how all the syllabi had a designated section for AI usage. Not only in our average day-to-day lives is AI prevalent, but in politics as well. Donald Trump has used AI many times throughout his second term. The one I remember most is him flying a plane over the #NoKings protests late last year, he showed himself decked in gold, flying and relieving himself over all the protesters. Another post I remember was when Soccer Star Cristiano Ronaldo and the King of Saudi Arabia visited the White House. Trump posted a video of himself and Ronaldo having a kickabout in the Oval Office. These videos are made to increase favor through humor or literally showcase how much he cares about his enemies. When Nesrine Malik said AI is “sensationalized online material,” my mind cast to Trump and Ronaldo in the Oval Office. The name "Slop" is very fitting: you get a quick video with one of the most popular people in the world, having fun and laughing with Donald Trump, and you see how cheap and low-effort the video you just watched is. Furthermore, AI is not only damaging to the environment, but it also distracts us from genuine issues going on around the world. Malik gave an example of her relative being fooled by videos emerging from Sudan being created by AI. She is one of the many victims (including myself) who have been overwhelmed and fooled by AI images. There is so much going on in the news, and a video of a member of Congress spouting racially charged dialogue followed up by a Cat singing a song, is all too common. As Malik said, you start to doubt if you can believe your eyes, but what else is there to believe AI and politics have been intertwined since the beginning. What can we use AI to create? (Computer-generated pornography)? What can we make people believe using AI(Trump and Ronaldo kicking a ball around)? Where do we place AI datacenters so we can get unlimited use of this AI(Poor and low-income areas)? AI has changed the face of media so much that it has genuine ramifications on real life. People posting AI-generated images of feasts from Gaza while under Israeli siege limits sympathy for and undermines the plight of the Palestinians. I said earlier how I have been fooled by AI before, and that serves to say how much I have become desensitized to it(despite not outwardly looking for it), but how dangerously realistic it has gotten. There are images made in a certain art style, like mentioned in the article, with the Studio Ghibli styled woman crying as shes being deported, but with photorealistic elements, real people, current events, and news. These are a dangerous set of things to unleash on the public, especially in these globally difficult times.

Photomontage

In my photomontage, I aimed to highlight the feeling of being caught between worlds. On the surface, my image looks like someone standing between the Great Pyramids and New York City. I chose these images because they represent me. I am the son of immigrants living in America. As much as I try to uphold the values of my family's country, I find myself growing up in a society that doesn't share them. I see people around me living lives my family warns me against. And when I see people from Egypt, it strikes me how different the reality of living here compared to there is. As a storyteller, I attempted to write a story about this a few years ago. A young man growing up in America sees how his family is ostracized and portrayed around him, choosing to reject all influences from his culture to be accepted. In the process of creating this character, I noticed how similar he was to me, and how being caught between what you are surrounded by and what is expected of you is a unique experience. In this montage, I used several blend options as well as perspective. I wanted to showcase how this person was sandwiched between two difficult places. They are a product of his surroundings, yet they are still grounded in ideals from across the globe. The skies of the images blended well initially, but I chose to highlight the difference between them even more, just to showcase how they contrast. The blend tool I used on the NYC skyline was called “Hard Light.” I used it because of how brilliant it looked. I decided on this because it gave me a “grass is not always greener” kind of feeling, which is what I wanted the viewer to feel.

Data Visualization

In Motherhood in Crisis, we learn about the trials women in Sierra Leone face during childbirth. As a medically underdeveloped country, Sierra Leone does not have highly trained nurses and doctors to help women give birth safely and without lasting damage. In the first story, we learn about Memunatu, who was critically injured during the birth process. After a few weeks of being sick and incontinent, she is taken to a women's clinic and helped by doctors. For the first time, women like Memunatu are treated professionally and are allowed to live semi-normally after being injured during childbirth. The second story is about a young girl named Heela who is raped on her way home from school. After going to the police, she was brought to a center where she received counseling and felt safer after her ordeal. Eventually, Heela was released, and she gave birth to her child. However, she had to give up on her dreams of becoming her village's first-ever nurse. In the third story, we learn about a mother who fears seeking medical care in favor of spiritual healing due to the Ebola outbreak. While pregnant, she is in so much pain and is forced to go to a hospital against her wishes. However, after her child is born, she sees how helpful they can be in spite of the scary Ebola memories. The coronavirus reignited that same fear in her, as she is now afraid to take her children to the hospital. In the final chapter, we learn the story of Tetteh, a woman imprisoned for killing her abusive husband in self-defense. While in jail, she suffered from limited medical supplies and a lack of medicine. After she gave birth (and is fearful of Ebola, as many of the women in this project were), she learned how helpful hospitals and medicine could be. She is estranged from her son as she is still in prison, and at the time of writing, she and her son have not been in contact. The Motherhood in Crisis project is a brilliant look into the bleak and uncertain lives of African women regarding healthcare. Ebola was a scary time for many African countries altogether, however. One of its lasting effects, as evident in this project, was cutting the trust in hospitals among the people of Sierra Leone. The project highlights major flaws in the healthcare and justice systems in not only Sierra Leone but also many parts of Africa. The stories of Tetteh and Heela (a woman imprisoned for defending herself from her abusive husband, and a schoolgirl who was raped) make it very clear how little is done to protect women in these countries. Their stories highlight the importance of an accessible health system and a fair and just justice system. Their stories are important in understanding the realities of many women in Sierra Leone who cannot access healthcare for whatever reason, whether that be distance, trust, or familial issues. These stories serve a purpose in understanding how important healthcare is and how its access cannot be limited.
